Speciation & Genetic Drift

How does one species become two? Explore geographic isolation, gene flow, and the outsized role of random chance in small populations.

Two populations are separated by a barrier. Population A lives on a dark forest floor — dark organisms blend in and survive. Population B lives in a sandy desert — light organisms blend in and survive. Watch the two populations evolve in opposite directions over time.1,2

Complete
How completely the geographic barrier blocks movement between populations. At 1 (Complete), no organisms cross — gene flow is zero and populations diverge freely. Reducing it allows organisms to cross, mixing alleles and slowing or reversing divergence.
0.40
How different the two environments are. At 0, both populations share the same optimum and drift randomly. At 1, selection strongly pulls Population A toward dark and Population B toward light — causing faster, more dramatic divergence.

Key insight: In a small population (N=10–20), alleles frequently drift to fixation or loss within 20–40 generations purely by chance — regardless of whether those alleles are helpful, neutral, or harmful. This is why inbreeding in small, isolated populations erodes genetic diversity and increases extinction risk.1,3

r-Strategists — "Quantity"1

High growth rate · Many offspring · Little parental investment · Short lifespan · Population size fluctuates widely

Insects Most fish Dandelions Mice

Strategy: Produce massive numbers of offspring quickly — most will die, but some will survive in unpredictable environments.

K-Strategists — "Quality"1

Low growth rate · Few offspring · High parental investment · Long lifespan · Population size near carrying capacity K

Elephants Humans Whales Eagles

Strategy: Invest heavily in fewer offspring, each with a high chance of survival. Works in stable, competitive environments near K.
