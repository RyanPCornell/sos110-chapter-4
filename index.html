    4.1 · What Do Living Things Have in Common?

    Scales of Life — From Cell to Biosphere

    Living things maintain greater order than nonliving things (which requires a constant input of energy), can reproduce, and have the capacity to evolve over generations.

    Cell → Tissue → Organ
    Groups of cells form tissues; tissues form organs and organ systems.
    Organism → Population
    A population is a group of the same species living in a given area.
    Community → Ecosystem
    Community = interacting populations in a place. Ecosystem = community + nonliving environment.
    Biome → Biosphere
    Biomes are regional ecosystems; together they form the biosphere.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.1 · Earth's Spheres

    The Four Spheres

    Earth's systems are grouped into four spheres that constantly exchange matter and energy. Life runs through all of them.

    Biosphere
    Everywhere life is found, from deep soil to the open air.
    Hydrosphere
    All of Earth's water: oceans, ice, rivers, groundwater, and vapor.
    Atmosphere
    The layer of air that surrounds the planet.
    Geosphere
    The solid, physical Earth: rock, soil, and the interior.

    Biosphere integrity (a planetary boundary) directly impacts species extinction rates.

    4.2 · How Does Life Evolve? — Darwin and Natural Selection

    Galápagos Finches and Adaptive Radiation

    Darwin's 1831 voyage revealed finch species on different Galápagos islands had diversified from a common ancestor — each beak adapted to the food available on its island.

    • Natural selection: traits that confer survival or reproductive advantages are more likely to be passed to offspring over generations.
    • Adaptive radiation: one ancestral species diversifies into many species; each adapted to a specific niche.
    • Habitat: the place an organism lives; it determines which traits natural selection favors.
    • Species: a group of closely related organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.2 · Common Ancestry and the Tree of Life

    All Life Is Related

    Birds and crocodiles shared a common ancestor about 240 million years ago. Phylogenetic trees map these evolutionary relationships using shared traits and DNA.

    ~240M
    years since birds & crocodiles shared an ancestor
    • A phylogenetic tree (cladogram) shows how species descend from common ancestors over deep time.
    • Shared characteristics (anatomy, embryology, and especially DNA) reveal evolutionary relationships.
    • The more recently two species shared an ancestor, the more genetic material they have in common.
    • Evolution is not a ladder toward 'higher' forms — it is a branching tree with no predetermined direction.

    Interesting read: “The Story of Birds” by Steve Brusatte

    4.3 · What Causes Population Traits to Change?

    Speciation, Gene Flow, and Genetic Drift

    • Speciation: subsets of a population diverge genetically until they can no longer interbreed.
    • Reproductive isolation: geographic, physical, or behavioral barriers that prevent interbreeding.
    • Gene flow: when populations mix, genetic differences shrink while overall diversity rises.
    • Genetic drift: random, chance-based changes in trait frequency (strongest in small populations).
    • Small isolated populations lose genetic diversity through drift and inbreeding.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.4 · What Shapes Biodiversity?

    Counting Life on Earth

    Over 2 million species have been described, but as many as 86% of land species and 91% of ocean species may still be undiscovered.

    86%

    91%
    of ocean species undiscovered
    • Species richness: the number of different species in an area. Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace noticed tropical climates hosted greater diversity than temperate regions.
    • Biodiversity has three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity.
    • Biodiversity benefits ecosystems by supporting adaption and stability.

    4.4 · Biogeography — Why Species Live Where They Do

    Geography Shapes Life

    • Biogeography studies the geographic distribution of species across the planet.
    • Tropical regions have greater species richness than other regions, a pattern called a latitudinal gradient.
    • Barriers like oceans, mountains, and deserts block gene flow and promote independent evolution.
    • Island biogeography: islands have fewer species than equivalent mainland areas, but high endemism — species found nowhere else.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.4 · Ecosystem Diversity and Biomes

    Climate Shapes the Biome

    Tropical Rainforest
    Warm and wet year-round; the greatest species richness on Earth.
    Temperate Forest
    Four seasons, moderate rainfall; deciduous trees and high productivity.
    Grassland / Savanna
    Seasonal drought limits trees; grazers and predators dominate.
    Desert
    Very low precipitation; organisms specially adapted to conserve water.

    Important: Global warming can decrease ecosystem productivity by altering species distributions and stressing organisms.

    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.5 · How Do Communities of Organisms Interact?

    Three Fundamental Interactions

    Competition
    Organisms vie for limited resources — interspecific (between species) or intraspecific (within a species). Losers may be excluded from a habitat. A negative–negative interaction.
    Predation
    One organism (predator) feeds on another (prey). Top predators can trigger trophic cascades that reshape entire ecosystems indirectly.
    Symbiosis
    A long-term close association: mutualism (+/+), commensalism (+/0), or parasitism (+/−). Often shaped by coevolution.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.5 · Predation and Trophic Cascades — Yellowstone Wolves

    How Top Predators Reshape Landscapes

    Removing wolves from Yellowstone in 1926 caused a trophic cascade: the elk population increased and the aspen tree population dramatically decreased.

    1926
    wolves removed
    1995
    wolves reintroduced

    When wolves were reintroduced in 1995, the following happened:

    • Wolves reduced elk numbers and changed elk behavior, keeping them out of vulnerable valleys.
    • Freed from overgrazing, willows and aspens recovered — stabilizing stream banks.
    • Beavers returned and built dams, creating wetlands; river channels narrowed and deepened.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.5 · Yellowstone Wolves and Trophic Cascades

    The Cascade by the Numbers

    Counts from Yellowstone's northern range put numbers on every link in the chain: predator, herbivore, plants, and the ecosystem engineer that followed.

    Wolves in the park
    number of wolves
    31 wolves were released in 1995 and 1996. The population peaked in 2003, then settled as wolves filled the available territory.
    Northern-range elk
    animals counted
    Elk fell by roughly two-thirds. Recent counts hold near 5,000 to 6,000, but wolves were only one of several causes.
    Aspen recruitment
    stands with aspen over 200 cm
    At 200 cm a young aspen is tall enough to escape elk browsing. In 1998 not one stand had reached it. Recovery was uneven: browsing fell to 37% in the east but stayed near 63% in the west.
    Beaver colonies
    colonies on the northern range
    With willows recovering along the streams, beaver returned, rebuilding dams, wetlands, and narrower, deeper channels.
    Read the numbers carefully. Wolves are one driver among several. Drought, bears and cougars, and elk hunting outside the park all pushed in the same direction, and ecologists still debate how much of the willow, aspen, and beaver recovery the wolves themselves caused. A correlation this striking is a starting point for investigation, not proof of a single cause.
    Elk counts: Northern Yellowstone Cooperative Wildlife Working Group annual winter counts. Wolf numbers: NPS Yellowstone Wolf Project annual reports. Aspen: Painter, Beschta, Larsen & Ripple, Ecology 96(1):252–263 (2015), 87 stands resampled in 2012 against a 1997–98 baseline. Beaver colonies: Smith & Tyers, Yellowstone beaver surveys (2012). Debate: Ripple & Beschta, Biological Conservation (2012); Marris, Nature (2014). Values are approximate.

    4.6 · What Controls Population Size?

    Growth, Limits, and Reproductive Strategies

    • Populations grow when births + immigration exceed deaths + emigration.
    • Carrying capacity (K): the maximum population an environment can sustainably support — growth follows a logistic (S-curve) pattern as it nears K.
    • Density-dependent limits (disease, competition, predation) intensify as populations grow.
    • r-strategists: many offspring, little care (insects, fish).
    • K-strategists: few offspring, high investment (elephants, humans).
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.6 · What Controls Population Size?

    Patterns of Population Growth

    Exponential
    When conditions for growth are ideal, populations can increase very quickly over time — known as exponential growth.
    Logistic
    More realistic: growth slows as the population approaches carrying capacity, tracing an S-shaped logistic curve.
    Population Crash (Boom–Bust)
    St. Matthew Island reindeer: introduced in 1944, they grew exponentially to 6,000 — then crashed to 42 when they exhausted their food.

    4.7 · What Can Cause Loss of Biodiversity?

    Extinction, Keystone Species, and Vital Areas

    Sea stars are a keystone species: when removed from intertidal habitats, mussels and barnacles outcompete other species — and biodiversity drops.

    • A human-driven sixth mass extinction is underway — the passenger pigeon went from the most abundant US bird to extinct.
    • Keystone species have an outsized influence on ecosystem structure relative to their abundance.
    • Biodiversity hotspots: areas with high endemism and severe threat — priorities for protection.
    • Main drivers: habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species, and climate change.
    © 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
    4.8 · What Can I Do?

    Supporting Biodiversity

    • Create wildlife-friendly habitat where you live. The National Wildlife Federation's certification program shows how.
    • Plant native species: they support native insects and birds that exotic plants cannot.
    • Reduce your negative impact on wildlife (reduce light pollution, keep track of your pets).
    • Learn your local biodiversity, reduce your footprint, and encourage others to do the same.
